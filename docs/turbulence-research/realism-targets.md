# Make severe turbulence a rare spike, not a level

Real airliner turbulence is mostly absent, sometimes light, occasionally moderate, and severe only as a few seconds of jolts inside a longer bumpy patch. At cruise, roughly **97% of time is smooth, about 3% is light or worse, about 0.4% is moderate or worse and about 0.1% or less is severe**. US airline data put a severe pilot report at roughly **one per 3,500 flight hours**, and a serious-injury turbulence accident at roughly **one per 1.8 million flight hours**. Measured injury events on widebodies, including a 747 (UA826), peak at **nz of about −0.8 to −1.5 g and +1.5 to +1.8 g**. The violent part lasts **0.6 to 6 s**, the altitude loss is only **100 to 180 ft**, and up to about 20 s of moderate-to-severe chop comes first. Measured against this, frankenweather's current SEV TURB SIGMET behaviour is too uneven. A randomly drawn level held for minutes produces either near-smooth air (10%, p95 0.03 g) or minutes of severe-class shaking with extreme-class peaks (55%, p95 0.53 g, max 2.1 g). A real SEV TURB SIGMET transit is mostly light and moderate, with severe present only as rare, short, localized cores. The recommended fix has four parts. Replace the "level per source" with a **patch-and-cluster model**. Target **about 10–25% of time at moderate and about 0.2% at severe inside a SEV TURB SIGMET**. That gives a **severe cluster on about one transit in five** and a true extreme event on roughly **one SIGMET transit in 2,000**. Finally, make frequency and maximum intensity crew multipliers on those rates rather than on the base level.

## Severe means Δg near 1 g for seconds, and extreme means negative g

No current regulation defines turbulence categories in g. The FAA describes them by aircraft and occupant behaviour: moderate leaves the aircraft "in positive control at all times", while severe brings "large, abrupt changes in altitude and/or attitude" and possibly momentary loss of control ([FAA Aviation Weather Handbook excerpt](https://data.ntsb.gov/Docket/Document/docBLOB?ID=19973276&FileExtension=pdf&FileName=FAA+Aviation+Weather+Handbook+-Turbulence+Excerpts-Rel.pdf)). The working g scale used by NCAR is **moderate >0.5 g, severe >1 g, extreme >2 g**. The same NCAR slides note that these values depend on the aircraft, and that peak g scales with ε^1/3, so roughly linearly with EDR ([Sharman, NCAR](https://cpaess.ucar.edu/sites/default/files/2023-08/Sharman-Bob.pdf)). ICAO Annex 3 Amendment 79 sets peak-EDR thresholds of **0.10/0.20/0.45** for light, moderate and severe. These apply to a *medium* transport, and heavy aircraft need a higher EDR to reach the same category ([ICAO WAFS training](https://www2023.icao.int/WACAF/Documents/Meetings/2025/Workshop-SADIS/2%20WAFS%20Gridded%20Data.pdf)). Weather-radar standards define "severe turbulence" as **0.3 g RMS** ([NTSB SS-21/01](https://ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/Documents/SS2101.pdf)).

The incident record shows where those thresholds sit on real widebodies. **UA826, a 747-122 at FL310 in clear-air turbulence, peaked at +1.81 g and then −0.82 g, lost about 100 ft, killed one unbelted passenger and injured 102** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Airlines_Flight_826); [ASN](https://aviation-safety.net/asndb/324068)). **SQ321, a 777-300ER at FL370 near developing convection, had about 19 s of nz between +0.44 and +1.57 g. Then nz fell from +1.35 g to −1.5 g within 0.6 s and returned to +1.5 g within about 4 s**, with a 178 ft altitude loss ([AirlineRatings, TSIB preliminary](https://www.airlineratings.com/articles/preliminary-singapore-airlines-turbulence-report)). Events that injure crew without negative g are smaller. A Delta 777 swung between +0.02 and +1.56 g over 10 s ([NTSB](https://data.ntsb.gov/carol-repgen/api/Aviation/ReportMain/GenerateNewestReport/82465/pdf)). Another 777 went from 2.2 g to about 0.5 g in 5 s ([NTSB WPR11LA203](https://data.ntsb.gov/carol-repgen/api/Aviation/ReportMain/GenerateNewestReport/78892/pdf)). A 30 s event ran between −0.17 and +1.62 g ([NTSB DCA15LA067](https://data.ntsb.gov/carol-repgen/api/Aviation/ReportMain/GenerateNewestReport/90744/pdf)). For scale, FAR 25 design limit loads are **+2.5/−1.0 g** ([14 CFR 25.341](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/14/25.341)). SQ321's −1.5 g briefly exceeded the negative limit.

| Category (747 at cruise) | Peak Δg at CG | nz range | Real signature | Single WxBurst size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Light | 0.05–0.2 | 0.8–1.2 | slight erratic bumps, drinks ripple | 20–40 |
| Moderate | 0.2–0.5 | 0.5–1.5 | AP holds, IAS varies, walking difficult | 40–63 |

| Extreme | >1.2–2.5 | negative g, down to −1.5 | SQ321/UA826 class, injury and fatality | needs paired opposite bursts |

Size and seat position change the picture somewhat. ICAO's "heavier needs higher EDR" statement and the classical gust-load formula suggest that a 747 at a given EDR feels about **one category notch lower, roughly 0.6–0.8 times the Δg of a medium jet**. This is an inference, because no published 747-specific EDR-to-g coefficient was found. The heavier aircraft also filters out short-wavelength gusts, so the same air feels more like slow heave. In the other direction, NTSB data show the **pilot station sees about 12% more Δg than the CG and the aft galley about 40% more** ([NTSB SS-21/01](https://ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/Documents/SS2101.pdf)). That matters for frankenweather's ButtKicker coupling through Vibrate. One caveat applies to the addon's own metric: altitude second differences smoothed over ±0.5 s will under-read a 0.6 s spike like SQ321's. A logged max of 2.1 g therefore points to a real peak at least that large.

The expected autopilot behaviour follows the same split. The 747-400 turbulence penetration speed is **290–310 KIAS / M0.82–0.85, whichever is lower** ([747 FCOM limitations](https://www.scribd.com/document/461296148/Operating-Limitations)). Boeing-derived guidance keeps the autopilot engaged and lets altitude vary ([B777 OM-B copy](https://files.bavirtual.co.uk/bavirtual-docstore/om-b/b777/Severe%20Turbulence%20and%20Windshear.pdf)). In NTSB severe convective cases, the autopilot disengaged by itself, stick shaker and overspeed warnings appeared, and the aircraft pitched down ([NTSB SS-21/01](https://ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/Documents/SS2101.pdf)). Moderate turbulence should therefore leave the autopilot holding within about ±50 ft. Severe turbulence should produce ±100–200 ft swings, IAS excursions of ±10–20 kt and an occasional disconnect. Minutes of autopilot altitude swings should not occur.

## Airliners meet severe turbulence about once per thousand-plus hours, and SIGMETs concentrate it only loosely

Williams calibrates cruise-level turbulence to **light-or-greater 3.0%, moderate-or-greater 0.4% and severe 0.1%** of the atmosphere, accurate to within about a factor of two. For a large transport that splits into null 97.0%, light 2.1%, light-to-moderate 0.5%, moderate 0.2%, moderate-to-severe 0.1% and severe 0.1% ([Williams 2017](https://d-nb.info/1130499898/34)). He summarizes this as about **30 s of severe turbulence on an average eight-hour flight**, where "1 in 10 flights" gets several minutes and nine get none ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/yes-airline-flights-are-getting-bumpier-heres-why/)). Airliners avoid much of that air, and their objective recorders show less. About **95% of Delta EDR values sit in the zero bin** ([NOAA TM GSD-53](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/16874/noaa_16874_DS1.pdf)), and DTN found **about 85% zeros** ([DTN](https://aviationdata.dtn.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/26/2023/03/The-DTN-Aviation-Numerical-Turbulence-Forecast-Overview-and-Verification.pdf)). In the US in-situ archive, EDR exceeds 0.5 in only **about 2 × 10⁻⁵** of records ([AMT 2022 citing Sharman et al. 2014](https://amt.copernicus.org/articles/15/2277/2022/)). With per-minute records, that is roughly one severe minute per 800 flight hours.

Pilot-report and accident counts give the same order of magnitude. The US logs about **65,000 moderate-or-greater and 5,500 severe-or-greater PIREPs a year** ([NCAR RAL](https://ral.ucar.edu/aap/turbulence)), against about 19.5 million Part 121 hours ([AvWeb](https://www.avweb.com/aviation-news/accidents-edge-up-in-2019-ntsb/)). That is about 3.3 MOG and 0.28 SOG reports per 1,000 airline hours, or **one severe report per roughly 3,500 hours**. GA reports inflate these rates and under-reporting deflates them. Part 121 had **111 turbulence accidents in 2009–2018, about 11 a year**, each with at least one serious injury ([NTSB SS-21/01](https://www.ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/Documents/SS2101.pdf)). That is about **0.05 per 100,000 hours** ([Aviation Week](https://aviationweek.com/business-aviation/safety-ops-regulation/turbulence-not-just-airline-problem)). Taken together, "felt severe" occurs about once per 1,000–3,500 hours, and the SQ321/UA826 class is roughly 500 times rarer than a severe PIREP. Convection was present in **58%** of those accidents and CAT in **29%**. Descent and approach account for about half, and **60% occurred below FL200** ([NTSB SS-21/01](https://www.ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/Documents/SS2101.pdf)).

Forecast areas raise these odds only modestly, so inside them smooth and light air should still dominate. US G-AIRMETs caught only **45% (EDR) to 55% (PIREP) of moderate-or-greater events**. Inside them, GTG could **halve the false alarms while keeping 80–90% of the hits**, which means most of the air inside an advisory is less than moderate ([NOAA TM GSD-53](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/16874/noaa_16874_DS1.pdf)). Severe turbulence is barely forecastable: GTG captures **10% or less of EDR >0.4** ([NOAA TM GSD-53](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/16874/noaa_16874_DS1.pdf)). NWS meteorologists say most turbulence SIGMETs are **issued only after a PIREP** ([NTSB SS-21/01](https://www.ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/Documents/SS2101.pdf)). A SEV TURB SIGMET therefore marks where someone was hit, not a volume full of severe air. Global EDR data quantify the enrichment: in the most strongly deformed jet flow, the moderate-or-greater fraction rises from **about 1% to about 8%** ([WCD 2025](https://wcd.copernicus.org/articles/6/1583/2025/)). That is an order-of-magnitude increase, but even there more than 90% of samples are below moderate. No published statistic gives the fraction of a SEV TURB SIGMET that is actually severe. The targets below are engineering estimates anchored to these enrichment factors, and the report flags them as such.

## Turbulence comes in minute-long patches containing seconds-long jolts

The minute-to-minute structure matters as much as the averages. Measured patch lengths are skewed. Over 150,000 miles of U-2 flying, **fewer than 50% of turbulent areas exceeded 10 miles and only about 1% exceeded 30 miles**, except over Japan, where jet stream plus mountain wave produced 150–200 mile areas ([Coleman & Meadows 1959](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19980228362/downloads/19980228362.pdf)). Project Jet Stream found half its patches were under 20 miles ([Stephens & Reiter 1966](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19670003687/downloads/19670003687.pdf)). At 747 cruise speed, that is a median patch of **about 1–2 minutes**. Larger turbulent *regions* are about **60–100 km long and 0.5–1 km deep** ([Sharman et al.](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/39241.pdf); [Sharman NCAR slides](https://www.eol.ucar.edu/sites/default/files/files_live/private/Sharman_CRITE_CAT_final.pdf)). Inside them, **the ratio of turbulent to smooth stretches runs from about 1:1 to 1:10** ([Veazey 1970](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19700022691/downloads/19700022691.pdf)). Patches also persist: one was tracked for about 45 minutes with unchanged intensity, and median persistence is about 6 hours. The same position and level should therefore give similar turbulence on a second pass.

Within a patch, real turbulence arrives as "bursts of relatively intense motion separated by areas of relative calm". In a NASA-sponsored simulator study, test pilots rated constant-intensity Gaussian turbulence "a little too continuous" and its realism fair to poor. They rated patchy models "about right", and **pilot-rated realism tracked patchiness, not RMS intensity** (correlation 0.07 between patchiness and RMS) ([NASA CR-152621](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19770013171/downloads/19770013171.pdf)). The accepted modelling approach multiplies a fast gust process by a slow random intensity envelope ([Reeves, DTIC AD0701735](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD0701735.pdf)). The envelope's patch-to-patch intensity is roughly truncated-Gaussian or log-normal ([NASA CR-152621](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19770013171/downloads/19770013171.pdf); [Sharman slides](https://www.eol.ucar.edu/sites/default/files/files_live/private/Sharman_CRITE_CAT_final.pdf)). The standard high-altitude scale length of **2,500 ft** ([MathWorks von Kármán](https://www.mathworks.com/help/aeroblks/vonkarmanwindturbulencemodelcontinuous.html)) puts most of the felt energy at **1–10 s periods** at cruise TAS. Severe events in accident reports are **5–30 s clusters**: +1.81/−1.38/+1.99 g with oscillation lasting 28 s ([ASN 356769](https://asn.flightsafety.org/wikibase/356769)), or +1.5 to −0.3 g in 5 s ([ASN 314621](https://asn.flightsafety.org/wikibase/314621)). These clusters sit inside a longer bumpy period. Steiner's gust spacing gives the density: about **0.09 mi inside convective echo against up to 0.73 mi in clear air near cloud** ([Steiner, NACA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19930085520/downloads/19930085520.pdf)). At cruise, that is a gust about every 0.5 s in a storm core and every 4–5 s near cloud. Translated into felt bumps, light means a small bump every few seconds. Moderate means bumps every 1–3 s with a larger one every 10–30 s. Severe means that background plus 2–5 large jolts in 5–30 s. These rates are derived, not directly measured.

## Each turbulence source has a different signature

Mountain wave at cruise is mainly an airspeed and pitch problem rather than a jolt problem. Operational criteria put **moderate wave at 350–599 ft/min and severe at ≥600 ft/min**, with altitude changes over 1,000 ft ([NWS Reno](https://www.weather.gov/media/rev/IWAWS/PDFs/2022/David%20Craft%20-%20Anticipating%20Winds%20Near%20Complex%20Terrain.pdf); [ATSB](https://www.atsb.gov.au/node/9936)). Vertical currents reach about **2,000 ft/min** in strong waves ([SKYbrary](https://skybrary.aero/articles/mountain-waves)). The cruise incidents are about speed. At Clariden, a mountain-wave wind change pushed an A340-600 at FL380 into **overspeed, an autopilot disconnect and a brief loss of control** ([SKYbrary](https://skybrary.aero/accidents-and-incidents/a346-en-route-clariden-switzerland-2018)). Bramberger et al. document a wave-induced stall warning at FL410 ([UCAR](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/vertically-propagating-mountain-waves-a-hazard-for-high-flying-ai/)). Violent turbulence comes from wave *breaking* and from low-level rotors ([NWS Honolulu](https://www.weather.gov/media/hfo/hpaws/Mountainous%20Environment%20Hazards%20to%20Rotary%20Wing%20Operations%20-%20Vitalie%20Gumeniuc.pdf); [BOAC 911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOAC_Flight_911)), which are rare. The addon's post-limiter MTW replay (p95 0.09 g, max 0.18 g) fits this "mostly smooth heave" picture. The earlier p95 of 0.69 g did not.

Convective turbulence extends well beyond the visible cloud. Research penetrations measured updrafts of **about 25 m/s**, with an isolated **63 m/s** gust reported secondhand ([NOAA](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/17638/noaa_17638_DS1.pdf)). The SQ321 cell had an updraft estimated at **8,000–9,000 fpm** ([Asia News Network](https://asianews.network/severe-turbulence-that-hit-singapore-airlines-flight-in-2024-not-detected-by-weather-radar-investigators/)). Two 757s met severe turbulence in clear air **about 20 km from any optically thick cloud**, driven by anvil outflow ([Fovell, Sharman & Trier](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/126190.pdf)). Moderate-or-greater probability stays **about twice background out to 70 km** from convection ([Heaton, citing Hitchcock et al. 2025](https://caps.ou.edu/reu/reu25/finalpapers/Heaton_FinalPaper.pdf)). The addon's "TS areas mostly calm unless close to a CB zone" is therefore broadly right for the area as a whole. It needs a graded halo, though: a small severe probability within about 20 km of a cell and above or downwind of tops, and doubled moderate odds out to about 70 km.

Low-level turbulence is the most *frequent* kind and rarely severe. Delta's EDR heartbeats are almost all below 0.1 except in the 0–10,000 ft layer ([NOAA TM GSD-53](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/16874/noaa_16874_DS1.pdf)), and **55% of MOG observations came from below 15,000 ft** ([NTSB SS-21/01](https://ntsb.gov/safety/safety-studies/Documents/SS2101.pdf)). Engineering models set σ at about **10% of the 20 ft wind**, roughly 1.5 m/s in 30 kt ([Modelica Dryden](https://doc.modelica.org/Modelica%204.0.0/Resources/helpWSM/Modelica/Modelica.Blocks.Examples.Noise.DrydenContinuousTurbulence.html)). Surface roughness matters more than stability ([AWI](https://epic.awi.de/41431)). This implies light chop mostly under 0.2–0.3 g in 15–25 kt winds over flat terrain, occasional 0.5 g bumps in 30+ kt winds or over rough terrain, and severe only in rotors or near convection. The testing-profile boundary layer's p95 of 0.02–0.03 g is plausible for light winds but probably too gentle for a windy day.

## Recommended frankenweather targets: patches, clusters and rates per hour

**Diagnosis of current behaviour.** The core problem is the architecture, not any single gain. One continuous percentage per source is drawn at random and held for minutes, and a burst rate ∝ level^1.5 then makes p95 and max g rise steeply above about 45%. Measured on 2026-10-25/26, the same SEV TURB SIGMET swung from **10% (p95 0.03 g, essentially light)** to **55% (p95 0.53 g, max 2.1 g)** within minutes. A p95 of 0.53 g means severe-class acceleration for roughly 5% of the time, around 3 s every minute, sustained for minutes. A 2.1 g peak exceeds UA826 and matches SQ321. That is tens of times more severe exposure than any real-world estimate supports, delivered as a "level" rather than as an event. The pre-limiter MTW result (p95 0.69 g) had the same cause: same-direction stacking. The limiter removed the stacking. The fix is to make severe and extreme *discrete, rare, scripted clusters*, and to cap the continuous background at moderate.

**Restructure each turbulence source as three layers.** The *region* layer is the advisory polygon or the terrain, CB or BL source. It sets the odds and never directly sets g. The *patch* layer is a deterministic, position-seeded field of patches. Patch lengths are log-normal with a median of about 1–2 minutes (15–30 km at cruise) and a tail to 10 minutes; wave and jet cases get 20+ minutes. Patches are 1,000–3,000 ft thick, so a 2,000 ft level change usually exits one. Each patch draws an intensity class (smooth/light/moderate) from the region's mix, and the same patch gives the same answer on a second pass. The *cluster* layer is a Poisson process of severe events, each 5–30 s long. A cluster starts with moderate chop, then fires 2–5 large opposite-sign bursts. Only the cluster layer may exceed burst size of about 60 (Δg about 0.45). The continuous background should be hard-capped at moderate (size ≤ about 60). Severe jolts should use size 63–97, and extreme should be a dedicated up-then-down pair that is allowed to bypass the same-direction limiter. A single burst cannot reach SQ321's −1.5 g, so an extreme event has to combine an up-burst and a down-burst within a few seconds.

**Default time fractions and event rates.** The cruise-background row comes straight from the measurements. The advisory rows are engineering estimates: the measured enrichment factors (about 8–10× for moderate in favoured flow, severe forecastable at ≤10%) applied with the design goal in mind. Rates are per hour of flight *inside* the area. "Severe cluster" means peak Δg 0.5–1.2. "Extreme" means peak Δg >1.2 with negative nz.

| Area type | Smooth | Light | Moderate | p95 Δg (whole transit) | Severe clusters / h | Extreme / h |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cruise, no advisory | ~95% | ~4% | ~0.3% | ≤0.03 | ~1/2,000 | ~1/500,000 |
| G-AIRMET / WAFS MOD, PIREP MOD | 40–60% | 30–45% | 5–15% | 0.10–0.20 | ~1/100 | ~1/50,000 |
| SEV TURB SIGMET | 30–45% | 35–45% | 10–25% | 0.15–0.25 | ~0.5 | ~1/1,000 |
| MTW SIGMET (cruise) | 40–60% smooth heave | 30–40% | 5–15% | ≤0.12, plus IAS ±10–20 kt and 350–1,000 fpm drafts | ~0.2 (breaking) | ~1/2,000 |
| CB within 20 km / above tops | 30–50% | 30–40% | 15–30% | 0.15–0.30 | ~0.5–1 | ~1/500 |

These numbers satisfy the design goal directly. At 0.5 severe clusters per hour, a typical 30-minute SEV TURB SIGMET transit has a **22% chance of a severe cluster** (1 − e^−0.25). The crew spends that transit mostly in alternating light and moderate patches, enough to put the seat-belt sign on, but usually without anything severe. Severe exposure inside the polygon averages about 0.2% of time (0.5 clusters/h × ~15 s). That is far below the notes' upper engineering estimate of 1–5%, deliberately, because severe is localized and largely unforecastable. Within severe clusters, the peak Δg distribution should follow the real-world ratio of injury encounters to severe reports: about **85% at 0.5–0.8 g, about 13% at 0.8–1.2 g and about 2% beyond**. True SQ321/UA826-class events should be about **1 in 500 severe clusters**, matching the roughly 11:5,500 ratio of serious-injury accidents to severe PIREPs. That puts extreme at roughly one per 2,000 SIGMET transits and roughly once per half-million hours in clean air: never zero, never likely. Make the SIGMET level random per *patch*, not per *source*. Bias the odds upward near the SIGMET's reported position and flight level, since most turbulence SIGMETs follow a PIREP. Keep TOP/BASE layering strict.

**Response targets to validate against in flight tests**, using the addon's existing ±0.5 s smoothed metric. Light patches: p95 0.03–0.10 g with the autopilot within ±20 ft. Moderate patches: p95 0.15–0.35 g, peaks up to 0.5 g, autopilot within about ±50 ft, IAS ±5–10 kt. Severe cluster: 2–5 peaks of 0.5–1.2 g within 5–30 s, ±100–200 ft, IAS ±10–20 kt, a possible overspeed clacker near M0.85, and an autopilot disconnect allowed only in clusters above about 0.8 g. Extreme: a single SQ321-shaped sequence, about 20 s of chop at ±0.5 g, then a 0.6–1 s drop to nz −0.5 to −1.5 and a rebound to +1.5–1.8 within 4–6 s, net altitude loss 100–200 ft. A 747 sits about one notch below published medium-jet values, so keep the table above as the 747 figures and do not inflate them. If seat effects use cockpit-station acceleration, scale the CG value by about 1.1.

**Crew-configurable options.** Expose two independent controls that act on the patch and cluster layers. **Frequency** should be a multiplier of 0.25×, 0.5×, 1× (default, the table above), 2× or 4×. It scales the share of moderate patches and the severe-cluster rate, with the 4× setting labelled as exceeding real-world rates. **Max intensity** should be Light, Moderate, Severe, or Extreme (default). It truncates the cluster-peak distribution without increasing it. A crew that wants "never extreme" can opt out, while the default keeps the realistic small tail the design goal requires. A third, optional **severity bias** could shift the cluster-peak distribution (for training a severe-turbulence procedure) while leaving frequency unchanged. Keep this separate so that "more often" and "worse" are never coupled, as they are now through the level^1.5 rate. Per-source enable switches (SIGMET, G-AIRMET, PIREP, MTW, CB, BL, shear) remain useful. Log every severe and extreme cluster with time, position, source and peak g, so crews and testers can confirm the rates over many flights.

## Conclusion

The research supports a clear model of realism: severe turbulence is something an aircraft *hits*, not something it flies *in*. Real data show a few seconds of violent motion embedded in ordinary moderate chop, inside patches a minute or two long, scattered sparsely through advisory areas that are mostly smooth or light. Pilot ratings back this up: simulator turbulence feels real when it is patchy and surprising, not when its RMS is high. Frankenweather's current per-source continuous level conflates frequency with intensity and lets dice rolls hold severe-class shaking for minutes. Moving the severe and extreme tail into discrete, rate-controlled clusters is the main change needed both to meet "not a roller coaster" and to keep a small real-world chance of a bad day.

Two uncertainties remain. No published source gives the fraction of a SEV TURB SIGMET that is actually severe, or a 747-specific EDR-to-g coefficient. The SIGMET, G-AIRMET and CB rows above are therefore calibrated estimates, not measurements. The fastest way to firm them up is the addon's own harness: fly many SIGMET transits at default settings, then check that about one in five contains a severe cluster and that the whole-transit p95 stays near 0.15–0.25 g.
